Though reading from the Sinaxadion for October 21st, on which we commemorate,
our righteous Father, Ilarion the Great, St. Ilarion was born in Palestine in the year 293,
in the village of Thabatha, not far from Gaza.
His parents, who were idolaters, sent him to pursue a literary education in Alexandria.
There he got to know Christians and discovered the heavenly teaching of the Gospel
that has made the wisdom of this world foolishness.
He heard much talk of St. Antony, who was renowned throughout Egypt,
and set out for the desert to visit him.
When he saw the angelic life that Antony led, he decided to stay at his side,
with the other disciples of the Father of Monks.
But as the crowds who flocked to the desert for his blessing prevented St. Antony
from attending to silent prayer, he decided to depart to the harsh wastes of the inner desert.
He gave Ilarion his tunic of horsehair and his coat of skin and sent the boy,
who was only fifteen, to lead the ascetic life with some companions in the desert,
around Mayuma, in the region of Gaza.
Ilarion boldly set off to engage in the struggle, where except for bands of robbers,
that from time to time passed through, no human life was ever seen.
There he set about bringing his body into subjection and fled youthful lusts
by a severe fast.
Fifteen figs eaten after sunset was all his daily food.
During the day he prayed, and was always singing Psalms,
as he worked the arid soil that produced weiriness from toil joined to fasting.
But nothing could be sold and tempt to avarice.
The devil assaulted thus in his own domain, attacked the youth as he had done St. Antony.
He appeared to him in the shape of wild animals and tried to frighten him with terrifying inexplicable noises.
But all this proved to be in vain, for the young man warded off his attacks by the sign of the cross,
and took the initiative in the warfare by mocking the impotence of the evil one.
From his sixteenth to his twentieth year, Ilarion's shelter was a simple cabin made of ball rushes and marsh grasses.
Afterwards, he built a little low cell that looked more like a tomb than a house.
He lay on the hard ground and washed and cut his hair only once a year on Easter.
He never washed the coat of his skin that St. Antony gave him and wore the same tunic until it fell to pieces.
He knew all of Holy Scripture by heart and recited it aloud, standing with fear as though God were visibly present.
From his twenty-first to his twenty-seventh year, a few lentils soaked in cold water was for three years his daily food,
and for the next three he took nothing but bread sprinkled with salt.
From his twenty-seventh to his thirty-th year, he lived on wild plants.
From the age of thirty to thirty-five, on six ounces of barley bread and a few vegetables cooked without oil.
Then, falling ill and with failing eyesight, he added a little oil to his food but did not increase his allowance of bread,
even though he saw his body growing weaker and believed his death was near.
At an age when others tend to decrease their austerities, he kept to this diet with redoubled fervor like a young novice until his death.
He never ate until after sunset and relinquished his fast neither for the greatest feasts nor for the greatest illnesses.
Through these labors beyond human strength that he undertook for love of God, not only was Eladion's heart opened to contemplation of heavenly mysteries,
but grace overshadowed his body also and gave him the power of working miracles for the consolation of the faithful.
He healed the sick and delivered many that were possessed by unclean spirits.
By the age of twenty-two, his renown had already spread throughout Palestine and beyond to Egypt and Syria.
Crowds of the faithful flocked to him and many wanted to embrace the angelic life at his side,
for until then the monastic life had not appeared in Palestine and Syria.
So Eladion became there what Saint Antony was for Egypt.
He kept up a correspondence with Saint Antony the Great,
who would say to people bringing their sick to him from those regions,
why do you give yourselves the trouble of coming so far when you have my son Eladion in your own country?
There were soon two thousand monks living around Saint Eladion as solidaries in their own cells,
and all taking him as their father and guide.
Once a year at the grape harvest, he would visit all the monasteries,
bringing their annual provisions and taking the opportunity of gathering together all his disciples.
By the time he reached the age of sixty-three, the multitude of brethren under his direction,
and the crowds of the sick and the faithful that were forever flocking to his desert from all quarters,
left him no time for silent contemplation.
He wept freely as he recalled the first years of his Askecies when he was completely unknown.
His tears prevailed upon his disciples to let him leave them,
but when the day came, more than six thousand wanted to follow him wherever he went,
so as not to lose the grace that was in him.
He succeeded in persuading them to go back and took with him only forty disciples, long journeys on foot,
fasting until sunset.
On hearing of Saint Antony's death, Eladion set off for Egypt to venerate all that Antony's presence there had made holy.
He cast himself to the earth, shedding abundant tears at all the places and before every object associated with a great older.
When he came out of Saint Antony's desert, Eladion went in search of solitude.
But, wherever he was, be it in the desert or in Alexandria,
he spread about him such grace, miracles and healings that his fame running before him,
he was besieged by crowds so as to have no rest.
During a three-year tyranny of Julian the Apostate, in the years 361 through 363,
Saint Eladion's monastery near Gaza was destroyed and his monks scattered.
The saint himself found refuge in Libya.
From thence he took ship to Sicily, expecting to find solitude in parts where he was unknown.
But, there too, he drew the crowds for compassion and love for mankind,
constrained him to drive out devils and to heal the sick.
He fled once more and made his way to a little town in Dalmatia,
still a barbarous region where he killed by his prayer a monstrous beast that was terrifying the natives,
and thus converted them to Christianity.
He fled by night to escape honors and embarked on a merchant vessel bound for Cyprus.
Scarcely had he set foot there, then possessed folk began to shout,
panic stricken that Eladion, the servant of Jesus Christ, had come to drive them from the island.
Since he needed a new hiding place, he went to an uninhabited part and found a cave,
very difficult of access on the top of a steep mountain.
He spent five years there, visited only by his faithful disciple, Esechios,
who came from time to time to give him news of Palestine.
When Eladion reached the age of 80, his body worn out by a lifetime of austere askees,
he prepared for his departure hence.
He called around him the few faithful who had been able to reach his cave,
and, keeping his eyes open, as he lay at the point of death, he said,
Go forth, O my soul. What do you fear? Go forth. Why are you disquieted within me?
You have served Jesus Christ for almost 70 years, and do you fear death?
When he had spoken these words, he gave up his soul to God,
and his disciples buried him immediately, as he had told them to do,
to avoid the honors the saints received when they are laid to rest.
Sometime afterwards, Esechios came to take the body of Saint Eladion with him to Palestine
so that it might be venerated by the multitude of his disciples.
Through the prayers of our Holy Father, O Lord, Jesus Christ our God,
have mercy on us and save us.
