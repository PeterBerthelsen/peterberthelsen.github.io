The reading from the Sinaxadion, for October 11, on which we commemorate, the 11th through
the 17th of October, Sunday of the Holy Fathers of the 7th Ecumenical Council, the 11th
to the 17th day of October.
On the 11th of the month, if it be a Sunday, or on the 1st Sunday thereafter, we commemorate
the Holy Fathers of the 7th Ecumenical Council that met at Nisea against the impious and
ignorant men that refuse to honor the holy icons and accuse of idolatry holy church
who venerates them with faith.
The 7th Ecumenical Council was convoked by the Empress Regent Irene and met at Nisea
in Bethinia from the 24th of September to the 13th of October in the year 787.
St. Tarasios, the patriarch of Constantinople, presided over 350 Orthodox bishops with the
addition of 17 others who renounced the iconoclast heresy during the sessions.
The monks so ferociously persecuted by the iconoclast Emperor Leo III, the Asarian in
the year's 717 through 741 and Constantine V Copronimos in the year's 741 through 775,
were strongly represented by about 136 of them who sat alongside the legates of the Pope
of Rome and of the patriarchs of Antioch and Jerusalem.
The Empress addressed the fathers setting out in carefully measured terms the reason for
their gathering.
The fathers then proceeded to anathematize the heretics who, under the pretext of idolatry,
had for almost 50 years forbidden Orthodox Christians to venerate the icons of Christ
and his saints.
So they put an end to the first period of iconoclasm but some years later, under Leo V the Armenian
in the year's 813 through 820, it recovered strength and was finally disposed of only in
843 through the zeal of the Empress Theodora and the patriarch Saint Mythodios.
The Holy Fathers anathematized the heretical patriarchs Anastasios, Constantine and Nikitas
demonstrated the errors of the pretended ecumenical council that Constantine V had gathered at Yeria
in the year 754 and proclaimed the eternal memory of the Holy Defenders of Orthodoxy.
The patriarch Yermannus, Saint John of Damascus, Gregory of Cyprus, and all those who were exiled
and tortured for defending the Holy icons.
At the end of the seventh and last session of the council, the fathers declared that, just
as representations of the holy and life-giving cross, so should the venerable holy images,
as well as the image of our Lord God and Savior Jesus Christ, as the image of our immaculate
sovereign lady the Mother of God, of the Holy Angels and of all the saints, whether represented
in paint, mosaic, or any other appropriate material, be placed in the holy churches of God,
on the sacred vessels and vestments, on walls, on boards, and in the streets.
The oftener, these pictured likenesses, are looked upon the more those who behold them
will be led back to remember their originals, and to turn to them, and to show forth by kissing
them a proper veneration, but not a true adoration that, according to our faith, is due to God alone.
But as incense and candles are offered in the honor of the figure of the precious and
life-giving cross, of the holy gospels and of other sacred things, so we do the same
in honor of the holy icons, according to the pious custom of the elders, for the honor
paid to the image, goes up to its prototype.
And whoever venerates an icon thereby venerates the person, Epostasis, who is represented
in it.
By so doing, we maintain the teaching of our holy fathers, and the tradition of the Catholic
church, which has proclaimed the gospel from one end of the world to the other.
It was not simply veneration of the holy images that the fathers defended in these terms, but
in fact, the very reality of the incarnation of the Son of God.
I represent the invisible God, wrote St. John of Damascus, not in as much as he is invisible,
but to the extent that he has become visible by sharing in our flesh and blood.
I do not worship matter, but I worship the creator of matter, who for me has become matter,
who has taken life upon himself in matter, and who through matter, namely his dead and
risen body, has accomplished my salvation.
In taking human nature upon himself, the word of God deified it without loss of the qualities
proper to it, which is why the image of Christ can be depicted, even though his glorified
human nature is beyond the reach of our senses.
And so the image of Christ that the faithful witness of the church has passed down to us becomes
the true presence of the divine and human person whom it represents, and a means of grace
and of sanctification for those who venerated with faith.
The second council of Nicaea is the seventh and last ecumenical council recognized by the
Orthodox Church.
This does not mean that there may not be ecumenical councils in the future, although,
in holding the seventh place, the council of Nicaea has taken to itself the symbol of perfection
and completion, represented by this number in holy scripture.
It closes the era of the great dogmatic disputes, which enabled the church to describe indefinitions
excluding all ambiguity, the bounds of the holy Orthodox faith.
From that time, every heresy that appears can be related to one or the other of the errors
that the church assembled in universal councils has unathematized from the first until the
second council of Nicaea.
Through the prayers of our holy fathers, O Lord, Jesus Christ our God, have mercy on us and save us.
