In the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, Amen.
The reading from the Sinaxadion for October 20th, on which we commemorate, the holy great
martyr Arthemios, Christ's holy, glorious martyr Arthemios, came of a noble family and
was highly regarded by the emperor Constantine the Great, who promoted him to the rank
of patrician and appointed him military governor, Dukes Agustalis, of Alexandria and of the whole
of Egypt, circa the year 330.
Arthemios did not allow his high responsibilities to weaken his faith or resolve to spread
the message of salvation wherever he went.
On the death of Constantine, the empire was divided between his three sons in the year
337.
Constantias, who inherited all the east, recited at Constantinople, he entrusted Saint
Arthemios with the task of bringing the relics of the Apostle Andrew from Patras in the Peloponnese
and those of Saint Luke from Thebes in Boitia to the Imperial City, where they were placed
in the Church of the Holy Apostles.
Some years later, when Constantias and his army were at Antioch, preparing for war with
the Persians, his cousin Julian took the opportunity of seizing the western part of the empire
that Constantias had been ruling as emperor since the year 351.
Constantias immediately set out to confront Julian, who had taken the imperial title, but
he died on the way to Selissia, with time only for baptism.
Julian then became emperor and straight away set about restoring idolatrous worship, imagining
himself to be a new prophet, chosen by the pagan gods for that purpose.
He sent decrees throughout the empire, ordering the restoration to idolatry of the temples
that had become churches under Constantine.
He restricted Christian academics in their freedom to teach.
He had the markets sprinkled with lustral water, so as to oblige citizens to eat food that
had, in this way, received a sort of idolatrous consecration, and in many parts of the empire,
new palms of martyrdom were won by victors in the contests of faith.
The apostate made Antioch his headquarters for carrying on the Persian war, and commanded
all provincial governors to meet him there with their forces.
In the order reached Arthemios, he got his army on the move and left Alexandria for Antioch,
knowing that the powers that be are ordained of God.
After the long journey, they reached the city where the disciples were first called Christians.
Just as the emperor had before him two priests, Ohenios and Makadios, whom he was intent
on forcing to renounce their faith for senseless cult of his gods.
They bravely countered all Julian had to say with a clear proclamation of the doctrine of
the redemption that God has wrought for us, making an end of words the emperor ordered them
to be struck five hundred times with rods.
Arthemios went up to the apostate, disgusted at what he saw.
He told him that the policy against the Christians he had set his hand to was of the devil and
would come to nothing because the arrogant power of the demons has been cast down by the cross
of Christ.
Furthermore, that the gods he worshipped under the names of Hermes or Apollo really amounted
to no more than the wood or metal their lifeless statues were made of.
The emperor's surprise gave way to fury when he learned that the man who dared to address
him thus was none other than Arthemios, the governor of Egypt, whom he suspected of having
compassed the death of his brother Gaius Constantine, the Caesar of the East, assassinated on the orders
of the emperor Constantius.
They seized Arthemios where he stood and tore off his badges of office and the emperor commanded
that he be brought before him the next day for the punishment appropriate to his offences.
They then bound the saint hand and foot, reigned lashes upon him and threw him badly bruised
into prison with Arthemios and Macarius.
Arthemios was full of joy and sent up his prayer to God, saying,
I thank the Lord for accounting me worthy to suffer torture for thy love and for numbering
me in the choir of thy saints.
The following day, Orenius and Macarius were exiled to the inhospitable confines of Arabia
where after a while they were beheaded.
The emperor had Arthemios brought before him and began by promising him high office in
the imperial household and even make him high priest of his gods.
If only he would give up the foolish beliefs of those called Galileans.
Offers of this kind could have no attraction for the saint who was already dead with Christ
to the vanities of this world.
He thought it would be pointless to enter upon an extended defense of Christianity before
the emperor, who as a youth had been brought up in the faith so he did know more than answer
the false accusations about his connection with the death of Gaius and assured the apostate
that nothing in the world could make him abandon the rock of the faith.
There was nothing the impotent tyrant could do except vent his rage through torture.
Red-hot skewers were run into the saint's body, but much to the disappointment of the emperor
Arthemios uttered not one cry or a single moan.
That evening in his cell Christ appeared to him and miraculously healed his wounds.
Strengthened by his vision, Arthemios spent fifteen days on his feet, neither eating nor
drinking but occupied day and night with prayer and the contemplation of the heavenly mysteries.
Meanwhile, Julian went to Daphne on the outskirts of Antioch, where he had the relics of saint
Babilas removed to one of the city cemeteries so that he could restore the cult of Apollo.
But one night, as the pagan priests were busy with sacrifices and asidious prayers that the
so-called God be graciously pleased to open his wood and metal mouth and deliver his oracle,
a fire came down from heaven which, without harming anyone, completely destroyed the temple
and the statue.
Julian blamed the Christians for this and stepped up persecutions throughout the empire.
New churches were closed.
At Sebast, in Samaria, the relics of the prophets Alisha and John the Baptist were consigned
to the flames.
At Cessaria, the statue of Christ set up by the woman that had an issue of blood was overturned.
In order to disprove Christ's prophecy of the end of sacrifices of the Old Law, the apostate
gave the Jews a grant of public money to rebuild the temple, destroyed in the year AD 70.
Amid widespread persecution of Christians, Julian had Artemios brought out of prison
to be put to death.
A large rock lying near the theater was split in two.
The saint was laid on one half and the other was brought down upon his body.
As the stone fell, everyone could hear his bones break.
He was left where he was until the next day, in the belief that he was dead.
But when they lifted the stone, the tyrant was astonished beyond measure to see this man,
broken bone, disemboweled and eyeless, still scorning idols and glorifying the cross of Christ.
He was brought down from repenting at the sight of this marvel, Julian wanted an end made
of Artemios and ordered his beheading.
The martyr heard the sentence with joy and lifted up his hands to heaven in thanksgiving and
prayer for the well-being of the church.
He made his way of his own strength to the place of execution, bowed himself thrice eastward
to the earth and offered his neck with joy to the Headsman's sword.
A pious noble woman got possession of the martyr's body and took it to Constantinople where
for centuries it was fervently venerated by the faithful and wrought miracles without
number.
Through the prayers of our Holy Father's O Lord, Jesus Christ our God, have mercy on us
and save us.
